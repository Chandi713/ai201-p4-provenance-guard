# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. *"The
detector is accurate"* is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. *"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"* is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. *"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"* is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next unit costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

---

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area | A question it could answer |
|---|---|
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI? |
| Score spread | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable? |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded? |
| Bad input | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous? |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

---
## 1. Human writing is rarely called AI

Of 10 pieces written by people (my own writing, or text published before 2020),
including 3 written in plain, simple language, **at most 1** gets the
high-confidence AI label (`guess` = `"ai"`). This must hold in each of the 3
trials.

"Plain, simple language" means short sentences (about 12 words or fewer on
average) using everyday words, the way a younger writer or someone writing in
a second language might write.

**Why this target:** On a writing site, a wrong accusation costs the writer far
more than a missed AI text costs the site, so only 1 in 10 is allowed. The 3
plain pieces are there on purpose: plain writing is exactly what signal one
mistakes for AI (my own plain test sentence scored 0.67). Making them 30% of
the set, with only one failure allowed, means the system can't pass by doing
well on the easy pieces. If two plain pieces are called AI in any trial, the
criterion is missed.

---

## 2. Clearly-AI and clearly-human text score apart

For 5 clearly-AI pieces (generated by a chatbot, unedited) and 5 clearly-human
pieces, the average `confidence` of the AI pieces minus the average
`confidence` of the human pieces is **at least 0.24**. This must hold in each of
the 3 trials.

**Why this target:** 0.24 is the width of my unsure band (0.53 to 0.77). A gap
at least that wide means that, on average, the two groups land on opposite
sides of the unsure band instead of both inside it. A smaller gap would mean the
score isn't telling the two apart.

---

## 3. All three labels are reachable

Using a fixed set of 9 inputs (3 clearly AI, 3 clearly human, and 3 borderline:
lightly-edited AI or plain human writing), each of the three labels
(high-confidence human, unsure, high-confidence AI) appears **at least once**.
This must hold in each of the 3 trials.

**Why this target:** Which label a piece gets depends on the input, so coverage
can't be promised, only tested. Each label gets 3 inputs aimed at it but only
has to appear once, so one or two poorly chosen inputs can't fail the criterion
on their own. If a label still never appears across all 3 inputs aimed at it,
that points to a gap in my score ranges, not bad luck, and a reader could never
see that message.

---

## 4. Every appeal is recorded and changes status

Of 10 appeals, 8 use a real `content_id` from an earlier submission and 2 use a
made-up one.
- **All 8** real appeals return `200`, leave the item with status
  `under_review`, and appear in `GET /log` as an appeal entry. A repeat appeal
  on an item already under review is still logged, and the status stays
  `under_review`.
- **Both** fake appeals return `404`, and no appeal entry is logged for them.

**Why this target:** The appeal is the only thing standing between a wrongly
accused writer and nothing at all. Unlike the scoring, it involves no model:
the same request always follows the same coded path, so the result can't vary
between trials. A single failure would be a bug, not bad luck, which is why
every appeal must pass. An appeal on an unknown id must fail loudly instead of
looking like it worked.

---

## 5. Bad input gets a clear error, never a crash

Of 7 malformed `/submit` requests (no JSON body or a body that isn't a JSON
object, `text` missing, `text` empty or only spaces, `text` under 20 words,
`text` over 10,000 characters, `text` not a string, and `creator_id` missing),
**all 7** return a `4xx` JSON error that says what was wrong, and **0** return
a `5xx`.

**Why this target:** A crash on malformed input is one of the most common real
failures, and it's the cheapest thing for an attacker to try. The writer should
always get a message that tells them what to fix. Like the appeal path, this
relies on no model: each malformed request is caught by a fixed check in code,
so the outcome is the same in every trial. Anything less than all 7 would be a
bug, not variation.
